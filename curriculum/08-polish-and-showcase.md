# Class 8 — Polish, Test and Showcase

**Big idea:** Finishing is a skill. Making something work for *someone else* is different from
making it work for you.

**This class is structurally different from the other seven.** The first half is a working
session, the second half is the showcase with families in the room. Read the run sheet in
`admin/showcase-run-sheet.md` alongside this plan.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **New concepts** | Debugging strategy, user experience, sharing and remixing, online citizenship |
| **New blocks** | None. This week is consolidation |
| **Class activity** | Find the Bug, then Playtest Swap |
| **Project checkpoint** | Finished, shared, demo-ready |
| **Homework** | None. A "keep going" sheet instead |

## Before the class

- Print `handouts/class-8.md` and the certificates.
- A deliberately broken Scratch project ready on the projector for Find the Bug. Break it
  yourself the night before: remove a `wait` from a `forever`, point a `touching?` at the wrong
  sprite, and leave out a `set score to 0`. Three bugs, increasing subtlety.
- The club **Scratch studio** created and ready for students to add projects to.
- Chairs, snacks, and the projector set up for families arriving at the halfway mark.
- Certificates printed and **names filled in** — do this the night before, not on the day.

## 0:00–0:10 — Show & Tell

Last one. Make it count — get four or five up rather than two, and prioritise anyone who hasn't
presented yet all term. Some children will have been quietly waiting for permission all course.

## 0:10–0:20 — Find the Bug (whole class)

Put the broken project on the projector. Don't tell them how many bugs there are.

Run it. Ask: *"What did you expect, and what did it do?"* Take suggestions from the floor and
try them — including wrong ones. Getting it wrong publicly and recovering is the demonstration.

As you go, name the three strategies on the board:

1. **Say what you expected and what happened.** Half of all bugs are found in this sentence.
2. **Change one thing at a time.** If you change three things and it works, you don't know why.
3. **Explain it out loud to someone.** Even to a rubber duck. You'll usually find it mid-sentence.

Ten minutes, three bugs, done. This is the most transferable thing in the entire course — it
applies to homework, to arguments, and to the rest of their lives.

## 0:20–0:35 — Playtest Swap

Everyone stands up and moves two seats to the right. You now play the game in front of you for
five minutes, **without asking its author anything**.

On the card provided (on the back of the handout):

- Two things you liked
- One thing that confused you
- One thing you'd add

Then move back and read your card.

The rule that makes this work: **the author may not defend their game.** They just read the card
and say thank you. Watching a child discover that their instructions made perfect sense to them
and no sense to anyone else is the entire lesson about user experience, and it needs no
explanation from you.

## 0:35–1:00 — Polish sprint

Fix what the playtest turned up, then work down the finishing checklist. Put it on the board:

1. **Title screen** — the game's name and the author's name
2. **Instructions** — one line, on the title screen. "Use the arrow keys to catch the mangoes."
3. **Score and lives visible** and readable
4. **Game over screen** showing the final score
5. **At least one sound**
6. **Credits** — their name, and the club

Be ruthless about scope. If a child wants to add a boss fight in the last twenty minutes, the
answer is no — write it on their "keep going" sheet for next week instead. A polished simple
game demos far better than an ambitious broken one, and there is no recovering from a game that
breaks in front of forty parents.

### Sharing

Walk them through **See Project Page → Share**, then adding the project to the club studio.

Take two minutes for the online citizenship conversation, properly:

- Anyone in the world can see a shared project. Don't put your full name, your school, your
  address, or a photo of yourself in it.
- Comments are public. Be kind. If someone is unkind to you, don't argue — report it and tell
  an adult.
- If you use someone else's sprite, sound or idea, credit them. That's what the credits screen
  is for. Remixing is encouraged on Scratch; pretending it's all yours isn't.

## 1:00–1:05 — Reset the room

Families arrive. Chairs out, projector up, snacks on the table.

## 1:05–1:30 — The Showcase

Full detail in `admin/showcase-run-sheet.md`. Structure:

- Two minutes of welcome from a volunteer. Keep it short — the parents are here for their kids.
- Each student demos for **two minutes**: what the game is, a quick play, and the hardest bug
  they fixed. That last question is the good one. It's what turns a demo into a story.
- Certificates.
- Ten minutes of open play where parents try the games and the kids explain them. This is the
  best part and it happens by itself.

**Have a fallback.** Wi-fi at community venues fails at exactly the wrong moment. Download every
student's project as an `.sb3` file to a USB stick during the polish sprint, and have Scratch
Desktop installed on the presenting laptop. If the internet dies, the showcase carries on and
nobody knows.

## No homework

Instead, everyone leaves with the **keep going** sheet: where to find more projects, how to keep
building, and what next term looks like.

Point them at:

- [More Scratch](https://projects.raspberrypi.org/en/pathways/more-scratch) — the natural next step
- [Further Scratch](https://projects.raspberrypi.org/en/pathways/further-scratch) — clones, My Blocks, boolean logic
- [Code Club Aotearoa projects](https://codeclub.nz/page/all-code-club-projects) — free, local, hundreds of them
- The club studio, so they can see what everyone else built and remix it

## Five project ideas for the holidays

On the keep-going sheet:

1. A two-player version of Sky Catcher — one on the arrow keys, one on WASD.
2. A quiz about something you love, with a score.
3. An animated version of a story you know.
4. A drawing app using the Pen extension.
5. A "guess the number" game where the computer picks and you guess.

## Notes for the second volunteer

Your job today is entirely logistical: certificates, the USB backup, chairs, and making sure
every single child gets their two minutes. Watch for the quiet one who says they don't want to
present — offer to stand next to them and ask the questions, which almost always works. Nobody
should leave without having shown their family something they made.

## Afterwards

- Message every parent within two days with the studio link, so they can show grandparents.
- Ask two questions in that message: what did your child like most, and would they come back?
  You'll want the answers when you plan term two.
- Write down what went wrong while you still remember it. You will run this again, and week-one
  you will thank week-eight you.
