# Program Overview

**Course:** KCC Code Club: Game Makers
**Ages:** 7–12, no prior experience assumed
**Length:** 8 classes × 90 minutes, weekly
**Class size:** 16 for the pilot term, 20 program ceiling thereafter
**Volunteers:** one per five students, never fewer than two
**Cost:** $40 for the eight-week term — $5 a session — collected up front

## What the students will be able to do by week 8

Build a working arcade game from a blank project, on their own, and explain how it works.
Underneath that: sequence, events, coordinates, loops, randomness, conditionals, boolean
logic, sensing, variables, message passing, operators, and their own custom blocks. That is
a real introduction to computer science, not a tour of a toy.

They will also have debugged their own code roughly two hundred times, which matters more
than any of the above.

## Staffing and class size

Size the class to the volunteers you actually have, not the other way round.

**One volunteer per five students. Never fewer than two adults in the room, whatever the
headcount. 16 students for the pilot term; 20 is the ceiling once the club has run once.**

If the group skews young — more than about a third of them are 7 or 8 — work to 1:4 instead.
A 7-year-old needs help reading the sheet, help dragging a block into a slot, and reassurance
roughly three times as often as an 11-year-old, and a room that's half 7s and half 12s will
feel understaffed at 1:5 even though the arithmetic says it isn't.

| Students | Volunteers in the room | Spares left, from a team of 5 |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 2 | 3 |
| 12 | 3 | 2 |
| **15** | **3** | **2** |
| 16 | 4 | 1 |
| 20 | 4 | 1 |

**Fifteen is the sweet spot for a team of five.** It's the largest class that still leaves two
people in reserve, and reserves are what get you through eight weeks — somebody will be sick,
travelling or dealing with a family thing, probably more than once. Sixteen is the cap rather
than the target.

### Why 20 is the ceiling, and why not in term one

The ratio governs how much *help* is available. It doesn't fix the three things that break in a
big room:

- **The projector demo.** Fifteen minutes of live coding only works if the back row can read the
  blocks. Past about twenty, they can't.
- **Pace.** One lead can hold maybe twenty children through a build. Beyond that it isn't one
  class, it's two classes sharing a lead, badly.
- **Devices.** Twenty working laptops, twenty spare mice, and venue wi-fi that survives twenty
  simultaneous connections. Wi-fi that copes with fifteen and dies at twenty-five is extremely
  common — test at the number you're actually planning for. Families bring their own laptops,
  so the club controls none of this; at twenty, one forgotten charger is a child with nothing
  to do for ninety minutes.

Hold 20 back until the club has run a full term, because at 20 you need four volunteers in the
room every week and that leaves almost no slack. Prove the reliability of your team first.

If demand exceeds the cap, **run a second cohort rather than a bigger room**, and use the
waitlist as the argument for it.

### What to do with volunteers beyond the minimum

In rough order of value:

1. **Roster spares rather than cramming everyone in.** A briefed substitute who has read the
   plans is worth more than an extra pair of hands in week one. Keep at least two in reserve
   if you can.
2. **Zone the floaters.** Give each floating volunteer a couple of tables rather than the whole
   room. Unzoned floaters gravitate to whoever is loudest, and the quiet stuck child still gets
   missed — which was the whole point of the extra staffing.
3. **Split the class by age.** With enough volunteers for two rooms, running 7–9 and 10–12
   separately is the single biggest quality improvement available to this programme. It removes
   the five-year spread, which is the hardest thing about a 7–12 room. It costs a second space,
   a second set of devices and double the prep — a term-two move unless the venue makes it easy.
4. **Rotate the lead.** Having two or three people who can run a session from the plan is what
   makes the club survive someone moving on.

### A note on the pilot

Deliberate under-loading is the right call for a first term. Twelve to fifteen students with
three volunteers and two spares will teach you what breaks, with enough slack to recover from it
in the room. Sixteen with four and no reserve teaches you the same things much more painfully.

## The class rhythm

Every class runs the same shape. Kids settle fastest into a predictable structure, and a
predictable structure is also what lets a substitute volunteer run a session from the plan.

| Time | Segment | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Arrival and Show & Tell | Two or three students demo last week's homework. This is the single strongest motivator in the whole program and it costs nothing. |
| 0:10–0:25 | New concept, live demo | You code on the projector and *deliberately make mistakes*. Watching an adult debug calmly is the lesson. |
| 0:25–0:55 | Class activity | Small, throwaway, everyone does it. Practising the concept with no risk to their real project. |
| 0:55–1:05 | Break | Non-negotiable. Ninety minutes of screen time without a break is not a thing 9-year-olds can do. |
| 1:05–1:25 | Project build | Add this week's layer to their own game. |
| 1:25–1:30 | Save, share, homework brief | Everyone saves and backs up. Nobody leaves with a broken game. |

**Fifteen minutes of talking, fifty of hands-on.** If you find yourself explaining for
twenty-five minutes, stop and let them build. They learn it in their fingers, not their ears.

## The long project

One game, **Sky Catcher**, built across all eight weeks. Full detail in
`long-project-sky-catcher.md`. Three design rules make it survive a volunteer program:

1. **It works at every checkpoint.** After every single class it is a playable, demo-able thing.
2. **Each week is one independent layer.** Miss week five and you can still do week six.
3. **A skeleton file exists at every stage.** A student who missed two weeks downloads the
   week-five skeleton, and they are back in the room. This is how you stop attrition
   compounding into dropout.

Students choose their own theme in week one — catching mangoes, stars, footballs, whatever
they want. The mechanics are identical; the ownership is not.

## Homework, bonus challenges, and the no-grading rule

Each week has **two homework tasks** and **three or four bonus challenges**.

Homework is short — fifteen to twenty minutes — and always applies something taught that day
to their own game. Bonus challenges are open-ended ("make it do X"), deliberately harder, and
exist to absorb the fast finishers who would otherwise be bored and disruptive.

Homework is never collected, never marked, and never chased. It is shown off voluntarily in
Show & Tell. A child who does no homework all term and still turns up every week is a success.
A child who feels bad about unfinished homework and stops coming is the failure mode you are
designing against.

## Differentiation, in practice

You will have a 7-year-old who has never used a mouse properly next to a 12-year-old who has
been on Scratch for a year. That five-year spread is the widest thing in the programme and it
is what's most likely to go wrong. Handle it like this:

- The class activity is the floor. Everyone does it.
- Bonus challenges are the ceiling. Nobody is expected to finish them.
- Pair the experienced kid with a beginner from week two and give them the title of "helper".
  Being asked to explain is the fastest way to actually learn, and it converts a potential
  disruption into a second floating volunteer.
- If someone is genuinely far ahead, hand them the Raspberry Pi Foundation
  [More Scratch pathway](https://projects.raspberrypi.org/en/pathways/more-scratch) and let
  them run. Do not hold them to the class pace.

### The 7-year-olds specifically

Dropping the floor from 8 to 7 is a bigger change than it looks, and it lands in two places.

**Reading.** The student handouts assume a competent reader. A 7-year-old often isn't one yet,
and a child who can't read the sheet will sit quietly and do nothing rather than admit it. Read
the activity steps aloud at the start of every activity block — all of them, every week, not
just when someone asks. It costs ninety seconds and it removes the problem entirely.

**Typing and mouse control.** Dragging a block into a small slot is fine motor work, and it is
genuinely harder at 7 than at 10. Spare USB mice stop being a nice-to-have and become essential.
Seat the youngest students where the floating volunteer passes most often.

If you end up with three or more 7-year-olds, seat them together rather than scattering them.
It's easier to give one table a slower pace than to chase four children around the room, and it
saves them from measuring themselves against a 12-year-old on every task.

## What to cut when you run out of time

You will run out of time. Roughly every second week. Cut in this order:

1. The second class activity (where there is one) — always optional.
2. Half the project build — push the remainder into homework and open the next class with it.

**Never cut** Show & Tell, the break, or the save-and-back-up at the end.

## Attendance and attrition

Expect to lose two or three students over eight weeks; that is normal and not your fault.
What is your fault is losing them because they fell behind. The skeleton files exist for
exactly this. If a student misses a week, message the parent with the skeleton link and one
sentence on what they missed, and they will be fine.

Free programs get flaky attendance. A fee collected up front measurably improves it — people
show up for what they have paid for. We charge **$40 for the term, which is $5 a session**:
enough that a family has decided to come, low enough that it turns nobody away. Collect it
before week one.

Agree with the committee, before you publish the fee, that no child is turned away over it and
who is allowed to waive it quietly. A waiver that needs a meeting is a waiver nobody asks for.

## Success measures

Do not measure test scores; there is no test. Measure these:

- **Completion rate.** How many of the students who started week one demo at the showcase? Aim above 75%.
- **Voluntary homework rate.** How many bring something to Show & Tell? Aim above half by week four.
- **Week nine.** How many are still opening Scratch a month after the course ended? This is the only measure that actually matters.
